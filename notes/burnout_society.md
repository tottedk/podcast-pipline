# Byung-Chul Han, *The Burnout Society* (research note)

**Edition used:** Byung-Chul Han, *The Burnout Society*, trans. Erik Butler (Stanford Briefs, Stanford University Press, 2015), ISBN 978-0-8047-9509-8. Originally *Müdigkeitsgesellschaft* (Matthes & Seitz, Berlin, 2010). The PDF scan is 69 pages; text pp.1-51 plus notes. Page numbers below are the printed pages (**p.N**). The whole text was read once.
**Form:** a short philosophical essay in eight parts: Neuronal Power (p.1), Beyond Disciplinary Society (p.8), Profound Boredom (p.12), Vita Activa (p.16), The Pedagogy of Seeing (p.21), The Bartleby Case (p.25), The Society of Tiredness (p.30), Burnout Society (p.35). It offers no empirical data or case studies; it argues by contrast and by reading other thinkers (Esposito, Baudrillard, Foucault, Ehrenberg, Arendt, Nietzsche, Benjamin, Agamben, Freud, Sennett, Handke, Melville, Kafka).

## Outline and key arguments
1. **Neuronal Power (pp.1-7).** Every age has signature illnesses: bacterial, viral, now "neurons". Depression, ADHD, borderline personality disorder and burnout are not infections but "infarctions" caused by an "excess of positivity" (p.1). The 20th century was "immunological" (inside vs outside, friend vs foe, Cold War logic); today otherness is being replaced by sameness/difference that provokes no immune reaction (pp.2-3). Violence now comes from within the system: "The violence of positivity does not deprive, it saturates; it does not exclude, it exhausts" (p.7). "Burnout syndrome occurs when the ego overheats" (p.7). He criticises Esposito and Baudrillard for forcing an immunological frame (pp.2-6).
2. **Beyond Disciplinary Society (pp.8-11).** Foucault's disciplinary society (hospitals, prisons, barracks, factories) is replaced by an **achievement society** (*Leistungsgesellschaft*) of gyms, office towers, banks and airports. People are no longer "obedience-subjects" but "achievement-subjects", "entrepreneurs of themselves" (p.8). The modal verb changes from *May Not / Should* to **unlimited *Can*** ("Yes, we can", p.8). "Disciplinary society ... produces madmen and criminals. In contrast, achievement society creates depressives and losers" (p.9). He rejects "control society" as still too negative (p.8). Continuity: Can serves the same drive to maximise production; "the achievement-subject is faster and more productive" (p.9). He critiques Alain Ehrenberg (*Weariness of the Self*): depression is not just "tired of having to become himself"; "it is not the imperative only to belong to oneself, but the pressure to achieve that causes exhaustive depression" (p.10); he also adds "impoverished attachment" (p.10). The depressive is an *animal laborans* that "exploits itself ... voluntarily", "predator and prey at once" (p.10). "'Nothing is possible' can only occur in a society that thinks, 'Nothing is impossible'" (p.11). "The exploiter is simultaneously the exploited" (p.11); "paradoxical freedom" (p.11).
3. **Profound Boredom (pp.12-15).** Multitasking is not progress but a regression to the wild animal's divided vigilance (p.12). Culture needs deep, contemplative attention; it is displaced by "hyperattention" (p.13). Walter Benjamin's "dream bird that hatches the egg of experience" = deep boredom (p.13). Walking vs running vs dancing: running is just accelerated walking; dancing is new (p.14). Cézanne, Merleau-Ponty, Nietzsche's warning of "a new barbarism" from lack of repose (pp.14-15).
4. **Vita Activa (pp.16-20).** Against Arendt's *Human Condition*: the modern *animal laborans* is not passive and anonymous, but "hyperactive and hyperneurotic" with "an ego just short of bursting" (p.18). Cause: loss of faith in duration; life is "bare", "radically fleeting" (p.18); the cult of health (p.18). Han claims we all carry "a work camp inside", "prisoner and guard, victim and perpetrator" (p.19). *(This section also compares depressed and burnt-out people to camp prisoners; the comparison is sensitive and is not used in the episode.)* Cato's/Cicero's "never more active than when doing nothing" (p.20).
5. **The Pedagogy of Seeing (pp.21-24).** Nietzsche: learn to see, to think, to speak; learning to see is not reacting at once to every stimulus (p.21). "Reacting immediately, yielding to every impulse, already amounts to illness and represents a symptom of exhaustion" (p.21). "Today we live in a world that is very poor in interruption"; acceleration "is abolishing all intervals" (p.22). We lose the capacity for **rage** (*Wut*), replaced by annoyance (*Ärger*); rage "interrupts a given state and makes a new one begin" (p.22-23). "Positive potency" (the power to do) vs "negative potency" (the power not to do); hyperactivity is "an extremely passive form of doing" (p.24). The computer "is stupid insofar as it lacks the ability to delay" (p.22).
6. **The Bartleby Case (pp.25-29).** Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener": Han reads Bartleby ("I would prefer not to") as a pathological, disciplinary-society case of apathy, not (as Agamben reads) pure potentiality (pp.25-28). The story is "not a tale of de-creation but ... of exhaustion" (p.29). Kafka's "Hunger Artist" (p.28-29).
7. **The Society of Tiredness (pp.30-34).** Achievement society becomes a **doping society**: "neuro-enhancement" (p.30). Peter Handke's *Essay on Tiredness*: **two tirednesses**: solitary, "divisive" I-tiredness that isolates and ends speech (p.31), vs "fundamental tiredness" or "we-tiredness" that opens a "between" ("I am tired with you", pp.31-33); it "tells them not so much what they should, as what they need not, be". Han links it to the Sabbath as a day of "not-to", free of "in-order-to" (pp.33-34). "Society of tiredness" as a hope (p.34).
8. **Burnout Society (pp.35-51).** Kafka's *Prometheus*: "The gods grew weary, the eagles grew weary, the wound closed wearily" (p.35); Han reads the eagle as the self's alter ego: self-exploitation (p.35). Freud's psyche is a disciplinary apparatus (superego, repression); the achievement-subject has no such repression; psychoanalysis cannot grasp depression (pp.36-42). Kant's conscience/duty subject; God rewards; today's subject has **no Other to give recognition** and so has a "crisis of gratification" and keeps performing more (p.38). Sennett and narcissism; no "closure" (pp.38-40). Depression vs mourning and melancholy; social networks' "friends" heighten narcissism (p.43). Ehrenberg: melancholy as exceptional, depression as "melancholia plus equality" (pp.43-44); Han argues melancholy is a phenomenon of negativity, depression of positivity (p.44). Violence of consensus (p.46, p.48). Superego (prohibits) becomes **ego ideal** (seduces); "the project turns out to be a projectile that the achievement-subject is aiming at itself" (p.47). "Auto-exploitation is significantly more efficient than allo-exploitation, because the feeling of freedom attends it" (p.47); "the victim of such violence considers itself free" (p.47). Against Agamben's homo sacer: the "sovereign of achievement society is the homo sacer of his own self" (p.49). Nietzsche's Zarathustra: hectic work is "escape and the will to forget yourself" (p.50). Capitalism "absolutizes survival", not the good life; "Health is the new goddess" (p.51); the achievement-subject is "too alive to die, and too dead to live" (p.51).

## Key themes
A. **Positivity vs negativity:** harm no longer comes from the Other but from too much of the Same.  B. **Self-exploitation as efficient freedom** (Can replaces Should; the project, not the prison).  C. **Attention and boredom:** hyperattention vs contemplation; the loss of pauses, rage, and boredom.  D. **Two kinds of tiredness:** exhausted I-tiredness vs restful we-tiredness (Handke).  E. **Narcissism and the missing Other:** no recognition, no closure, gratification crisis.  F. **Bare life and the cult of health/doping.**  G. **Method and limits:** an essay of assertion, with no data; it sets itself against Foucault, Agamben, Ehrenberg, Esposito and Arendt.

## Advocate section (defending Han: exhaustion is self-inflicted; the achievement subject exploits itself)
The book's own best arguments: (1) the **paradox**: when nobody forces you, failure has only one author; (2) auto-exploitation is more efficient for capital because it feels like freedom (p.47); (3) the missing Other: no one tells you "enough", and no one can reward you either (p.38), so you do more; (4) the **loss of pauses**: boredom, rage and interruption are the conditions of thinking and experience (pp.13, 22); (5) tiredness need not be a disease: Handke's we-tiredness and the Sabbath show a good tiredness (pp.31-34); (6) the cult of health and doping show the logic reaching the body (pp.30, 51).

## Antagonist section (achievement culture is freedom and agency; burnout is sentimental or an old complaint)
**From the text itself:** (a) **No evidence:** the book is an essay with no data; its history (disciplinary to achievement) is a clean schema that real societies don't follow neatly; (b) Han himself admits that melancholy existed in antiquity (p.44) and that Ehrenberg dated depression to the shift to self-responsibility, so "new" is contested; (c) Han reduces depression to one cause and dismisses psychoanalysis (p.41); (d) he reads Bartleby against a rich tradition (pp.25-28); (e) his picture has no politics beyond a hope for a "society of tiredness" (p.34).
**From outside (see list below):** structural objection (many workers are compelled by rent, debt and precarity, not self-exploitation); historical objection (acedia, neurasthenia, Seneca: the complaint is old); agency objection (effort can be meaningful: flow, self-determination theory); material objection (work is far less physically harsh than in the disciplinary factory age; Keynes predicted shorter hours).

## Well-known, verifiable outside material for the experts (certainty marked)
**Certain (well established):**
- Han is a Korean-born philosopher based in Berlin; other books: *The Transparency Society*, *Psychopolitics* (neoliberalism exploits freedom and data), *The Agony of Eros*, *The Disappearance of Rituals*.
- **Foucault**, *Discipline and Punish* (1975): disciplinary institutions (prisons, barracks, schools, hospitals). **Deleuze**, "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990): control replaces discipline; continuous modulation, the corporation replacing the factory. (Han rejects the label "control society" as still too negative, p.8.)
- **Ehrenberg**, *La Fatigue d'être soi* (1998; English: *The Weariness of the Self*, 2010): depression as the illness of a society that demands autonomy and initiative.
- **Max Weber**, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905): worldly asceticism, work as a calling; the "iron cage".
- **Karl Marx**: alienation; wage labour compelled by need. **Keynes**, "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" (1930), predicted a 15-hour week.
- **Burnout:** Herbert Freudenberger used "burn-out" in a 1974 paper; **Christina Maslach** developed the Maslach Burnout Inventory (exhaustion, cynicism or depersonalisation, reduced efficacy). **WHO ICD-11** (adopted 2019) classifies burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.
- **Old complaints:** *acedia*, the "noonday demon" of early Christian monks (Evagrius Ponticus, 4th century); **neurasthenia**, popularised by George Beard in 1869 (*American Nervousness*, 1881); **Seneca**, *On the Shortness of Life* (the busy waste life).
- **Jonathan Crary**, *24/7* (2013): sleep as the last barrier to round-the-clock capitalism. **Mark Fisher**, *Capitalist Realism* (2009): "depressive hedonia", the privatisation of stress. (Earlier episode link: Fisher on Mike Ma's irony.)
- **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi**, *Flow* (1990); **Deci and Ryan**, self-determination theory: autonomy, competence and relatedness drive wellbeing.
- **Literature:** Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853); Kafka, "A Hunger Artist" (1922) and the short text on Prometheus; Handke, *Versuch über die Müdigkeit* (1989).
- **Everyday:** "hustle culture"; "quiet quitting" (viral in 2022); the "right to disconnect" law in France (2017); four-day-week trials in Iceland (2015-2019).
- **Earlier episodes:** Heidegger's *das Man* (the crowd that tells you what to do); Land's accelerationism (speed as an end); Mike Ma's diagnosis of irony and nihilism; uploaded intelligence (the fantasy of a mind that never tires).
**Uncertain (do not use as fact):** that Han owns no smartphone (an interview anecdote); the details of Han's doctoral work; precise working-hour statistics; any claim that attention spans have fallen by a number (the often-quoted "goldfish" claim is widely reported as unsupported, which may be used as a debunk only); specific burnout prevalence figures; exact dates of Han's later prizes.

## Why this cast of arguments
The advocate defends Han's paradox as lived experience (everyday, email at midnight, the inner boss). The antagonist is not a bully of the tired; he defends agency, effort and the long history of the complaint, and he can press a real weakness: Han offers a diagnosis with no data and little politics. The disputed questions follow from this clash.
